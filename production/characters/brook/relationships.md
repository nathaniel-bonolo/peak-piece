# Brook: Relationships

## His original crew

Brook's grief begins with **his own** crew.

They are not the Rumbar Pirates.

Their death sequence can preserve Binks' Sake as the song that continues until nobody is left to play it.

## The dead crews

Brook develops parasocial relationships with people he knows only through wrecks, bodies, possessions, and logs.

This is compassionate, lonely, funny, and eventually unhealthy.

The dead become the community he builds because living company is unavailable.

## Rumbar Pirates

Brook knows the Rumbar crew after their deaths through what they left behind.

He becomes a witness to their love for Laboon rather than secretly being part of the promise from the beginning.

## Laboon

The Straw Hats have their own Laboon promise.

Brook should not hijack it.

His contribution is different:

> **I knew the people who loved you.**

That gives Laboon information and mourning rather than magically returning the exact crew member the story already connected to him.

## Straw Hats

The crew becomes evidence that Brook's second life is allowed to contain new relationships rather than only obligations inherited from the dead.

They help mostly by living around him rather than trying to repair him. Luffy drags him into present-tense chaos. Sanji makes dinner for the people actually there. Usopp eventually asks about Brook instead of only the stories Brook carries. Franky can help preserve remains respectfully without treating permanent physical carriage as mandatory.

### Robin

Robin is especially useful because she understands living among dead histories.

She is not Brook's therapist.

She can listen without pretending the dead are currently present. A tiny correction such as:

> **Didn't like storms?**

can do more than a speech when Brook says a dead friend **doesn't** like storms.

She helps Brook move from speaking **for** the dead toward remembering them accurately.

## Charlotte Linlin

No Linlin relationship is locked here.

The contested version is no longer interesting merely because "Big Mom killed Brook's crew." Its potential is thematic: Linlin believes what she claims should remain hers, while Brook has spent decades treating his second life as something owed to the dead.

If accepted, she becomes the external claimant to the same life Brook has also failed to claim for himself.
